Wall and Drywall Water Damage in Tyler

Drywall acts like a wick. Paper and gypsum pull water up from the floor line, so the wet zone frequently ends a foot or more past the highest point the water itself touched. From the room, the surface may seem dry and sound even though the hidden face and the insulation are saturated. Looks cannot be trusted here, so every wall gets tested with a meter.

Not every wet wall has to be torn out. When the water was clean and the drywall remains firm, drying it where it stands is often possible, at times by pulling the baseboard and pushing air into the cavity through small holes. When the wall is soft, crumbling, moldy or was soaked by dirty water, we make a flood cut, a straight line above the damage, and remove everything below it.

What It Looks Like

  • Cool, slightly clammy drywall next to walls at normal temperature
  • Trim along the floor that has puffed up, twisted or come loose
  • Carpet edges or floorboards wet and discolored right next to the wall
  • Fasteners and joint lines becoming visible on a once smooth wall
  • Soft or crumbly spots you notice when pushing lightly on the wall
  • Wall coverings lifting, blistering or creasing close to floor level
What We Do

How We Handle Wall and Drywall Water Damage

1

Map the Moisture

Using meter readings and thermal images, our team outlines where the water went, upward and sideways, on both faces of the wall and in neighboring rooms.

2

Decide What Stays

Based on the water category, how long it sat and how the drywall feels, we tell you plainly which sections can be dried in place and which need to come out.

3

Open Up or Air Out

Walls we are keeping have their baseboards removed so air can be pushed into the cavity. Walls that cannot be kept get a flood cut along a level line, with the wet insulation pulled.

4

Drying and Reconstruction

Dehumidifiers and air movers stay on, usually 3 to 5 days, until the studs and the drywall left in place test dry. The rebuild follows with fresh insulation, drywall installation and finishing, trim and a coat of paint.

Hidden Risks

Risks That Are Easy to Miss

Spread Into Adjoining Rooms

Water passes under the bottom plate into the next room or closet. We check both sides of every affected wall and the flooring along it.

Growth Behind the Paint

Mold thrives on the rear face of drywall because it is paper covered, dark and has no moving air. That side may be coated in growth even as the room side appears spotless.

Wet Sheathing Outside

Outside walls have sheathing past the insulation that can also get soaked. It gets a moisture reading prior to closing, since it holds dampness a long time.

Hidden Behind Cabinets

Wet drywall behind cabinets, vanities and built-ins gets no airflow and is easy to overlook. Checking under the cabinet base and on the reverse face tells us if it is wet.

Why Speed Matters

How the Damage Progresses

First hours

Water Starts Climbing

The bottom edge of the drywall takes in water from the floor and it begins moving up. Both the baseboard and the bottom plate start absorbing water.

Hours 24 to 48

Swelling and Blisters

The wet line rises higher, baseboards swell and paint begins to blister. Growth is able to get going on the paper that lines the hidden side.

Days 3 through 7

Panels Lose Strength

Paper peels from a core that is no longer firm. A musty odor grows stronger while the batts settle and mold covers more of the unseen face.

After a week

Framing Is Affected

Studs and plates stay wet long enough to grow mold and begin to decay. The amount of removal and reconstruction grows considerably.
